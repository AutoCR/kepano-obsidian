One-line takeaway: **VAD argues that end-to-end autonomous driving can plan more safely and efficiently by replacing dense rasterized scene representations with sparse vectorized agents, maps, and ego trajectories.**

# Core takeaways
- VAD is an end-to-end autonomous driving planner built around **vectorized scene representation**.
- Its main target is the weakness of dense BEV raster representations such as semantic maps, occupancy maps, flow maps, and cost maps.
- The paper represents the scene as explicit geometric structures:
  - dynamic agents as **motion vectors**,
  - road layout as **map vectors**,
  - the ego plan as an **ego trajectory vector**.
- Planning uses query interaction plus explicit geometric constraints, rather than dense occupancy reasoning or hand-designed post-processing.
- On nuScenes open-loop planning, the paper reports better L2 error, lower collision rate, and faster inference than UniAD.

# What “vectorized” means here
In this paper, **vectorized** does not mainly mean SIMD computation or a generic neural embedding.

It means representing scene elements as **sparse geometric vectors / polylines / trajectories** instead of dense pixels.

For example, a lane divider is represented as a sequence of points:

```text
[(x1, y1), (x2, y2), (x3, y3), ...]
```

A surrounding vehicle's future motion is represented as a trajectory:

```text
[(x_t1, y_t1), (x_t2, y_t2), (x_t3, y_t3), ...]
```

This is different from a rasterized BEV grid, where the same information is encoded as occupied / semantic pixels.

# Model architecture
VAD has four major stages:

1. **Backbone**: multi-view images are encoded into BEV features.
2. **Vectorized scene learning**: agent queries and map queries learn dynamic-agent and road-structure information.
3. **Planning inference**: an ego query attends to agent and map queries, then predicts the ego trajectory.
4. **Planning training constraints**: explicit vectorized losses regularize the plan.

# Planning Transformer
The **Planning Transformer** is the module where the ego query gathers vectorized scene information before the final trajectory decoder.

It is not a large standalone planner. It is mainly an ego-query cross-attention block over agent and map queries:

```text
ego query
  ↓ attends to agent queries
updated ego query with dynamic-agent information
  ↓ attends to map queries
updated ego query with dynamic + static map information
  ↓ MLP planning head + driving command
single ego trajectory
```

More concretely:

1. **Ego-agent interaction**
   - The ego query is the attention query.
   - Agent queries are keys and values.
   - This gives the ego query information about nearby dynamic agents and their motion context.

2. **Ego-map interaction**
   - The updated ego query then attends to map queries.
   - This gives the ego query lane, boundary, and road-structure information.

3. **Planning head**
   - The final planning head uses the updated ego-query features, optional ego status, and high-level driving command.
   - The command is one of: **turn left**, **turn right**, or **go straight**.
   - The head is a simple MLP-based decoder that outputs the future ego trajectory.

The ego planning output is **single-mode**:

```text
V_ego ∈ R^{T_f × 2}
```

So VAD does **not** output multiple ego trajectories with probabilities. The planner is command-conditioned, but it is not a multimodal ego planner like later trajectory-scoring or diffusion planners.

The multi-modality in VAD is for **surrounding-agent motion prediction**, not ego planning:

```text
V_agent ∈ R^{N_a × N_k × T_f × 2}
```

where `N_k` is the number of motion modes per agent. For the ego-agent collision constraint, VAD uses the highest-confidence predicted trajectory for each agent. The paper itself notes that better use of multimodal agent predictions for planning is future work.

# Planning constraints
VAD's distinctive planning supervision comes from three explicit constraints:

## Ego-agent collision constraint
The ego trajectory should maintain safe longitudinal and lateral distance from predicted surrounding-agent motion vectors.

This replaces part of what an occupancy map would usually provide for collision avoidance, but only for detected/predicted agents.

## Ego-boundary overstepping constraint
The ego trajectory should stay away from predicted road boundaries.

This uses the vectorized map directly instead of asking the planner to infer boundaries from a dense semantic map.

## Ego-lane direction constraint
The ego trajectory should align with nearby lane-divider direction.

This helps regularize the future ego motion to follow road structure.

# Comparison with UniAD
UniAD is also end-to-end and query-based, but its main paradigm is different.

| Aspect | UniAD | VAD |
|---|---|---|
| Main philosophy | Planning-oriented full-stack driving | Vectorized scene representation for planning |
| Core question | Which perception and prediction tasks should be coordinated toward planning? | What representation should the planner use? |
| Main representation | Query-based task interfaces, plus dense occupancy prediction | Explicit vectorized agents, maps, and ego trajectory |
| Planner support | MotionFormer + OccFormer + planner | Agent/map query interaction + vectorized constraints |
| Strength | Comprehensive task orchestration | Efficiency and explicit geometric planning constraints |

My concise view:

> **UniAD says: organize the whole autonomous-driving task stack around planning. VAD says: represent the scene itself as vectors, not dense raster maps, so planning becomes simpler, faster, and more geometrically constrained.**

# Is UniAD already vectorized?
Partly, but not in the same sense.

UniAD uses **queries** for agents, maps, motion, occupancy, and planning. That makes it object/query-centric. However, VAD's use of “vectorized” is more specific: the final scene elements used for planning are explicit geometric vectors such as agent trajectories, lane polylines, boundaries, and the ego trajectory.

So:

```text
UniAD: query-based and planning-oriented
VAD: query-based plus explicitly vectorized scene geometry
```

# What if UniAD removes MotionFormer and OccFormer?
Removing OccFormer would make UniAD closer to VAD because it removes dense occupancy reasoning.

But removing MotionFormer would also remove the module that predicts per-agent future trajectories. That makes it **less** like VAD in dynamic-agent reasoning, because VAD depends heavily on vectorized agent motion.

A better statement is:

> UniAD without OccFormer, but with a vectorized motion module and VAD-style geometric planning constraints, would become close to VAD. Simply removing both MotionFormer and OccFormer gives a lighter query-based planner, not VAD.

# Obstacle reasoning without occupancy
VAD does not fully solve general open-set obstacle detection without occupancy.

It handles obstacles through:

- detected agents and their predicted motion vectors;
- road boundaries and lane/map vectors;
- collision, boundary, and direction constraints.

This works well for benchmark-style traffic participants and road layouts, but it is weaker for irregular or open-set obstacles such as:

- debris,
- cones,
- fallen boxes,
- animals,
- construction barriers,
- unknown static objects.

A dense occupancy or freespace module can naturally mark “this region is occupied” even when the object category is unknown. VAD's occupancy-free formulation is therefore efficient and structured, but its safety depends on whether the relevant obstacle is captured as an agent vector or map vector.

For a real deployment, I would treat VAD as a strong planning core, but still add an independent safety layer:

```text
VAD-style vectorized planner
+ occupancy / freespace / unknown-obstacle detector
+ emergency braking or safety monitor
```

# Results noted in the paper
On nuScenes open-loop planning, the paper reports:

| Method | Avg. L2 ↓ | Avg. collision ↓ | FPS ↑ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| UniAD | 1.03 m | 0.31% | 1.8 |
| VAD-Tiny | 0.78 m | 0.38% | 16.8 |
| VAD-Base | 0.72 m | 0.22% | 4.5 |

The paper claims VAD-Base improves over UniAD by:

- 30.1% lower average planning displacement error;
- 29.0% lower average collision rate;
- 2.5× faster inference.

Caveat: the reported FPS comparison is not perfectly apples-to-apples because the paper reports UniAD on A100 and VAD on RTX 3090.

# My understanding
VAD is best viewed as a **representation-level refinement of planning-oriented end-to-end driving**.

It does not mainly propose “more tasks” or a larger full-stack system. Instead, it says that the planner should consume structured geometric objects directly. This makes the model more efficient and gives the planner explicit constraints that are easy to interpret.

The tradeoff is that occupancy-free planning can be brittle for unknown or irregular obstacles. VAD is elegant for agent-lane-road reasoning, but a practical autonomous-driving stack would likely still need a general occupancy / freespace / safety fallback.
