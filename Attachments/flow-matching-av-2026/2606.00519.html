Abstract

We present DriveAnchor, a three-stage framework for autonomous driving planning that achieves behavioral diversity, controllability, and safety in a composable pipeline. Demonstration Flow Pretraining replaces the unstructured Gaussian prior with a vocabulary of 2,398 trajectory shapes constructed by farthest-point sampling, structurally grounding behavioral diversity in vocabulary coverage. Guided Flow Post-training jointly post-trains an Energy Field module with FM—conditioning EF on static road geometry alone—to relocate anchors toward user-specified corridor polygons before flow generation, adding controllability without differentiable guidance; after Stage 2, new corridor presets require only EF updates, not FM retraining. Reward-Refined Flow Fine-tuning applies zeroth-order RL to align each anchor’s output with collision-avoidance objectives: because fθf_{\theta} is a deterministic feedforward network in single-step mode, each anchor uniquely determines the output trajectory, reducing reward optimization to a direction search in anchor space without log⁡πθ\log\pi_{\theta} computation or ODE-to-SDE conversion. Evaluated on approximately 2 million held-out driving scenarios, DriveAnchor reduces near-range collision rates by 89% and improves mean reward by 32% without degradation in imitation accuracy, with 2.06 ms inference on NVIDIA Drive Orin. DriveAnchor has been validated through real-world vehicle testing (Figure 4), confirming its practicality for production deployment.

1 Introduction

Flow matching [23, 44] has emerged as a strong generative backbone for autonomous driving planning, offering deterministic, efficient, one-step inference. Yet deploying these models in safety-critical settings exposes three gaps: (i) no structural diversity guarantee—without a structured prior, rare maneuvers silently collapse at test time; (ii) no real-time controllability—a deployed FM planner cannot be steered toward a target corridor without expensive retraining; (iii) imitation-only training cannot optimize safety—supervised FM reproduces training distribution tail behaviors [35, 12] and cannot directly optimize collision avoidance. Together, these gaps prevent a single generative model from being production-ready across the full operational spectrum of driving demands.

Addressing all three gaps simultaneously proves difficult. Existing controllability mechanisms [16, 52] require either gradient-compatible continuous rewards or full FM retraining whenever corridor specs change—both impractical at production deployment. Safety alignment via RL is further blocked: standard policy gradient requires log⁡πθ​(x)\log\pi_{\theta}(x), which is analytically intractable for flow matching’s deterministic ODE [24], and driving rewards are non-differentiable (per-scene collision detection), ruling out the differentiable reward models assumed by prior RL fine-tuning work [3, 49, 48]. No prior method addresses all three gaps without significant model approximation or retraining overhead.

DriveAnchor makes three contributions:

A composable three-stage framework for production deployment. Demonstration Flow (diversity), Guided Flow (controllability), and Reward-Refined Flow (safety) are fully decoupled: each stage adds one property without disrupting the others, and each is independently updatable—new corridor presets require only retraining EF; updated safety objectives require only rerunning Stage 3, without touching the rest of the pipeline.

A geometry-driven controllability module trained independently of the reward signal. The Energy Field (EF) operates before flow generation, relocating anchors toward user-specified corridor polygons using only static road geometry—no differentiable guidance, no scene-specific reward required. Different driving behaviors (lane keep, turns, lane changes) are encoded by varying the polygon and exit edge, making EF a reusable, preset-based layer that leaves FM and Stage 3 intact.

Zeroth-order RL fine-tuning of flow matching without log-likelihood. Since fθf_{\theta} is deterministic in single-step mode, anchor x0x_{0} uniquely determines the output trajectory. The FPS-constructed vocabulary provides a kinematically feasible, angularly diverse finite-difference basis in trajectory space—enabling reward optimization as a direction search in anchor space with no log⁡πθ\log\pi_{\theta} computation, no ODE-to-SDE conversion, and no differentiable reward signal required—applicable to any black-box safety oracle, including hand-crafted collision rules and learned safety classifiers.

2 Related Work

Generative planning and structured priors.

Diffusion models [15, 40, 18, 51, 20, 32] and flow matching [23, 2, 45, 44] have advanced trajectory generation in autonomous driving and robotics—for example, Janner et al. [18] showed that diffusion models can plan flexibly over long horizons, and Tan et al. [44] applied flow matching to achieve state-of-the-art results on nuPlan. End-to-end planners [17, 19, 33, 10, 8] also achieve strong results—UniAD [17] unified perception and planning in a single transformer—but all remain purely imitative. Scene encoding via vectorized representations [13, 22] and multi-sensor fusion [26] provides the perceptual backbone for these planners. Prior anchor-based methods [5, 50, 14, 46, 29, 38, 39, 54, 30, 55] use anchors as scoring primitives for post-hoc mode selection—e.g., MTR [38] combines intention-based anchors with a transformer for joint multi-agent prediction—not as generative priors. DriveAnchor instead uses FPS-constructed anchors as structured initial conditions for the flow ODE, structurally grounding behavioral diversity in vocabulary coverage.

Controllable generation.

Ho and Salimans [16] proposed classifier-free guidance for diffusion models by interpolating conditional and unconditional score estimates at inference. Zhong et al. [53] extended this to driving by injecting differentiable cost signals into the diffusion sampling process to steer traffic trajectories. More broadly, differentiable cost injection [52, 1] and in-training conditioning require either gradient-compatible rewards or full model retraining whenever corridor specs change—both impractical at deployment. DriveAnchor’s Energy Field is post-trained jointly with FM as a separate stage, operates on static road geometry, and requires no differentiable signal.

RL fine-tuning of generative models.

Ouyang et al. [31] showed that reward-aligned fine-tuning via RLHF substantially improves language model behavior; Shao et al. [37] further scaled this with group-relative policy optimization [37], establishing the paradigm of optimizing non-differentiable rewards over discrete outputs. Black et al. [3] adapted this to diffusion models by framing denoising as an MDP, but as with ReinFlow [49] and DanceGRPO [48], all of these methods require log⁡πθ​(x)\log\pi_{\theta}(x), intractable for flow matching’s deterministic ODE [24]. Offline sequence modeling [6] and robot control via Diffusion Policy [9] sidestep policy gradients but cannot optimize non-differentiable rewards. Malladi et al. [27] showed that ZO can fine-tune billion-parameter LLMs at inference-level memory overhead; DriveAnchor instead applies ZO directly in output trajectory space, exploiting anchor identity to naturally avoid distributional assumptions and ODE approximations.

3 Method

Figure 1 illustrates the full DriveAnchor pipeline. The framework is composed of three decoupled stages. Stage 1 (Section 3.2) pretrains the Flow Matching network fθf_{\theta} with a structured anchor prior—replacing an unstructured Gaussian with a vocabulary of 2,398 FPS-constructed trajectory shapes—to ground behavioral diversity in vocabulary coverage. Stage 2 (Section 3.3) jointly post-trains an Energy Field (EF) module that conditions on static road geometry to relocate anchors toward a user-specified corridor before generation, adding run-time controllability without FM retraining. Stage 3 (Section 3.4) applies zeroth-order RL to fine-tune fθf_{\theta} for collision avoidance: because fθf_{\theta} is deterministic in single-step mode, each anchor uniquely determines the output trajectory, reducing reward optimization to a direction search in anchor space. At inference, the scene encoder first encodes context; EF then proposes a corridor-guided anchor displacement and rematches it to the nearest vocabulary anchor to keep the decoder input in distribution; FM then generates one trajectory per anchor, and top-KK candidates are passed to a downstream cost selector.

3.1 Preliminaries: Flow Matching for Planning

Flow matching [23, 25, 2, 7] learns a velocity field vθ​(xt,t)v_{\theta}(x_{t},t) that transports a prior p0p_{0} to an expert distribution p1p_{1} along straight linear paths by minimizing:

ℒFM​(θ)=𝔼t,x0,x1​[‖vθ​(xt,t)−(x1−x0)‖2]\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0},x_{1}}\!\left[\bigl\|v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)-(x_{1}-x_{0})\bigr\|^{2}\right] (1)

where t∼Uniform​(0,1)t\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1) and xt=(1−t)​x0+t​x1x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}, offering more efficient inference than diffusion models [15, 42]. At inference, integrating vθv_{\theta} from t=0t{=}0 to t=1t{=}1 is deterministic; we denote the resulting map as fθ​(x0,s)f_{\theta}(x_{0},s): the same starting point x0x_{0} and scene context ss always yield the same output x1=fθ​(x0,s)x_{1}=f_{\theta}(x_{0},s)—a property Stage 3 exploits for its zeroth-order direction search.

DriveAnchor’s key departures.

Rather than sampling x0x_{0} from an unstructured Gaussian prior, DriveAnchor uses a structured anchor prior x0∈𝒜x_{0}\in\mathcal{A}: a vocabulary of 2,398 kinematically feasible trajectory shapes covering distinct maneuver classes, built by FPS over 100M+ real-world frames. Since every GT trajectory lies close to some anchor, the residual Δ​x=x1−x0\Delta x=x_{1}-x_{0} is small, enabling a single-step approximation x^1=fθ​(x0,s)\hat{x}_{1}=f_{\theta}(x_{0},s) with 𝒪​(|Δ​x|2)\mathcal{O}(|\Delta x|^{2}) error—analogous to the single-step inference of consistency models [41]—and empirically, two sequential passes (denoted FM*2; notation defined in Section 4) further reduce min-ADE. The model is also trained without explicit tt input, as described in Eq. (3).

3.2 Demonstration Flow Pretraining

The FM independently learns p​(GT∣anchor)p(\text{GT}\mid\text{anchor}) for each of M=2,398M{=}2{,}398 anchors built by iterative farthest-point sampling [34] over a historical corpus ℋ\mathcal{H} of 100M+ frames:

𝒜=FPS​({ak∗}k=1|ℋ|,M),ak∗∈ℝT×2\mathcal{A}=\text{FPS}\!\left(\{a^{*}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{H}|},\;M\right),\quad a^{*}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 2} (2)

The corpus is temporally disjoint from training and evaluation data. During training, for each scene a single anchor is randomly sampled from 𝒜\mathcal{A} and the model is trained on a noise-interpolated input:

xt=(1−α)​x~0+α​GT,α∼Uniform​(0,1)x_{t}=(1-\alpha)\,\tilde{x}_{0}+\alpha\,\text{GT},\quad\alpha\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1) (3)

where α\alpha plays the role of tt in Eq. (1) with x~0\tilde{x}_{0} as the structured anchor prior (replacing the Gaussian x0x_{0}); the model predicts Δ​x=GT−xt\Delta x=\text{GT}-x_{t} without knowing α\alpha, and at inference α=0\alpha{=}0 so fθ​(x0,s)f_{\theta}(x_{0},s) directly recovers GT−x0\text{GT}-x_{0}. At inference, the full vocabulary of MM anchors is evaluated simultaneously in a single forward pass, producing MM candidate trajectories. The anchor table additionally serves as the reference set for Stage 3 ZO gradient estimation, where its FPS-uniform coverage ensures diverse finite-difference directions across all maneuver classes.

Anchor density.

M=2,398M{=}2{,}398 anchors cover diverse driving behaviors with uniform spatial coverage (Figure 1), keeping GT trajectories close to the vocabulary and improving FM mode precision. The same density also benefits Stage 3 by providing usable finite-difference directions; with M≈250M{\approx}250, RL made little progress due to poor 160-D coverage.

Retrieval with ϵ\epsilon-ball constraint.

Naive nearest-neighbor retrieval can cause mode collapse in sparse anchor regions: a generated trajectory with no close same-mode neighbors may draw anchors from structurally unrelated maneuver types, conflating gradient signals across modes (see Appendix G.2, Figure 7). We address this by combining KNN with an ϵ\epsilon-ball filter: a candidate aja_{j} is admitted only if it is among the NN nearest by distance and satisfies ‖ai−aj‖2≤ϵ\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon, where ϵ\epsilon is the per-cluster median inter-anchor distance. This keeps the effective neighborhood within a single maneuver mode regardless of local density. We define the neighborhood of aia_{i} as:

𝒩​(ai)={aj∈𝒜|‖ai−aj‖2≤ϵ∧aj∈N nearest of ​𝒜}\mathcal{N}(a_{i})=\left\{a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}\;\Big|\;\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\;\wedge\;a_{j}\in\text{$N$ nearest of }\mathcal{A}\right\} (4)

We use a pre-built KD-tree index over 𝒜\mathcal{A} for 𝒪​(log⁡M)\mathcal{O}(\log M) lookup, enabling sub-millisecond neighborhood retrieval during Stage 3 training (N=16N{=}16 in all experiments; see ablation in Section 4.3).

3.3 Guided Flow Post-training

Stage 1 provides no controllability mechanism. The Energy Field (EF) fills this gap before Stage 3: a learned module that predicts a residual displacement, jointly post-trained with FM (with ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} as regularizer and encoder frozen), taking the scene encoding, a corridor polygon query, and the current anchor as input and outputting Δ​vEF∈ℝT×2\Delta v_{\text{EF}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 2} to relocate anchors before generation. A polygon specifies the region the trajectory must traverse and the exit edge, encoding different maneuvers (lane keep, turns, lane changes) without retraining the base FM or re-running Stage 3.

At inference, EF and FM are applied as a two-step pipeline:

a~EF=aanchor+Δ​vEF,arematch=arg⁡mina∈𝒜⁡‖a~EF−a‖2\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\text{EF}}=a_{\text{anchor}}+\Delta v_{\text{EF}},\quad a_{\text{rematch}}=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\|\tilde{a}_{\text{EF}}-a\right\|_{2} (5)
afinal=fθ​(arematch,s)\displaystyle a_{\text{final}}=f_{\theta}(a_{\text{rematch}},\,s) (6)

where aanchor∈𝒜a_{\text{anchor}}\in\mathcal{A} is the selected anchor, Δ​vEF\Delta v_{\text{EF}} is the EF displacement correction, and ss is the scene context. EF produces a continuous corridor-guided proposal, which is projected back to the nearest vocabulary anchor before FM decoding to preserve in-distribution inference and kinematic quality. The resulting two-step process can be applied iteratively, with each FM output serving as the next anchor.

Design rationale.

EF and FM operate on orthogonal signals (static geometry vs. dynamic agents); keeping them separate avoids FM retraining on every preset update. Conceptually, ‖Δ​vEF‖≈0\|\Delta v_{\text{EF}}\|\approx 0 means the anchor is already inside the corridor [21].

Training objective.

A trajectory a=(a1,…,aT)a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T}) is good if it enters the target polygon 𝒫\mathcal{P} and exits only through the designated edge ℰk\mathcal{E}_{k}:

good(a)=𝟙[∃t:at∈𝒫]∧𝟙[no exit through ∂𝒫∖ℰk]\text{good}(a)=\mathbb{1}\!\left[\exists\,t:a_{t}\in\mathcal{P}\right]\wedge\mathbb{1}\!\left[\text{no exit through }\partial\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{k}\right] (7)

EF is trained with a regression loss where the target displacement is:

δ∗​(a)={𝟎if good​(a)anear-good−anoisyotherwise\delta^{*}(a)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{if }\text{good}(a)\\ a_{\text{near-good}}-a_{\text{noisy}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} (8)

where anoisya_{\text{noisy}} is the noise-perturbed anchor input and anear-gooda_{\text{near-good}} is the nearest good trajectory from the clean vocabulary. Good trajectories are preserved (zero correction); bad trajectories are pulled toward the nearest good trajectory. Classification is purely geometric and scene-obstacle-independent, so EF provides no collision-avoidance guarantee. The exit edge ℰk\mathcal{E}_{k} encodes the target maneuver—switching only this parameter selects straight driving, turns, or lane changes without retraining; a passable-segment variant additionally handles lane changes with a hard-end ahead. Stage 2 applies six kinematic losses to FM output afinala_{\mathrm{final}} only: speed, acceleration, curvature, jerk, lateral acceleration, and lateral jerk (Appendix D). Six representative polygon presets are shown in Appendix G.1.

3.4 Reward-Refined Flow Fine-tuning

Stage 3 optimizes θ\theta to maximize:

J​(θ)=𝔼s∼ρ,x0∼𝒜​[R​(s,fθ​(x0,s))]J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho,\;x_{0}\sim\mathcal{A}}\!\left[R\!\left(s,\,f_{\theta}(x_{0},s)\right)\right] (9)

where ρ\rho is the training scene distribution and RR is a black-box collision reward. Since fθf_{\theta} is deterministic, ∂a/∂θ\partial a/\partial\theta is available via backprop; the challenge is computing ∂R/∂a\partial R/\partial a, which is intractable because RR is non-differentiable. We estimate it via zeroth-order finite differences over the anchor table, requiring only that reward differences are directionally informative—a condition satisfied by collision detection. This requires fewer assumptions than FlowGRPO (which needs a Gaussian SDE approximation) and SafeDiffuser [47] (which requires differentiable CBFs). The full policy update rule is given in Eq. (13).

Step 1: Nearest Neighbor Selection.

Sample anchor k∼Uniform​({1,…,M})k\sim\text{Uniform}(\{1,\ldots,M\}); generate query ai=fθ​(x0(k),s)a_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{0}^{(k)},s). Retrieve 𝒩​(ai)\mathcal{N}(a_{i}) via Eq. (4).

Step 2: Reward Querying.

Query ri=R​(s,ai)r_{i}=R(s,a_{i}) and rj=R​(s,aj)r_{j}=R(s,a_{j}) for all neighbors. Using raw anchors is valid by anchor-proximity (aj≈fθ​(aj,s)a_{j}\approx f_{\theta}(a_{j},s)), saving NN forward passes. The reward is defined as:

R​(s,a)=min⁡{t:collision at step ​t},or ​T+1​ if collision-freeR(s,a)=\min\bigl\{t:\text{collision at step }t\bigr\},\quad\text{or }T{+}1\text{ if collision-free} (10)

Setting the no-collision value to T+1=81T{+}1{=}81 (not T=80T{=}80) creates a one-step margin at the safety boundary, sharpening the gradient signal.

Step 3: Gradient Estimation.

We assign exponentially decaying weights by distance rank:

wi​j=exp⁡(−rank​(aj∣ai)N−1)w_{ij}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\text{rank}(a_{j}\mid a_{i})}{N-1}\right) (11)

and aggregate reward-weighted finite differences:

g^i=∑j=1Nwi​j⋅rj−ridi​j2+ϵ0⋅(aj−ai)∑j=1Nwi​j\hat{g}_{i}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}\cdot\frac{r_{j}-r_{i}}{d_{ij}^{2}+\epsilon_{0}}\cdot(a_{j}-a_{i})}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}} (12)

where di​j2=‖aj−ai‖22d_{ij}^{2}=\|a_{j}-a_{i}\|_{2}^{2} and ϵ0>0\epsilon_{0}>0 is a small numerical stability constant. Each term is a one-sided finite-difference estimate of the directional derivative of RR; the weighted sum reduces variance by aggregating reward differences across NN directions.

Step 4: Policy Update.

Treating g^i\hat{g}_{i} as a stop-gradient constant, the RL loss and combined training objective are:

ℒRL​(θ)=−1|ℬ|​∑i∈ℬsg​(g^i)⊤​ai,ℒ​(θ)=ℒFM​(θ)+λ​ℒRL​(θ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\text{sg}(\hat{g}_{i})^{\top}a_{i},\qquad\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}(\theta) (13)

Unlike SPSA [43] or ES [36], directions (aj−ai)(a_{j}{-}a_{i}) come from the FPS anchor table, guaranteeing kinematic feasibility and angular diversity across maneuver shapes.

GridMap-accelerated collision detection.

To evaluate N+1N{+}1 trajectories efficiently, we rasterize obstacles into a coarse grid and apply per-trajectory early termination; see Appendix C for details.

3.5 Training Pipeline and Inference

The three stages execute sequentially and are fully decoupled: Stage 1 trains fθf_{\theta} on ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} with the encoder pre-trained and fixed; Stage 2 jointly post-trains fθf_{\theta} and EFϕ\text{EF}_{\phi} on ℒFM+ℒEF+ℒkin\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{EF}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}} (encoder frozen) and fixes ϕ\phi after convergence; Stage 3 fine-tunes only fθf_{\theta} via ℒFM+λ​ℒRL\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} with ϕ\phi frozen. The anchor table 𝒜\mathcal{A} remains fixed throughout. Algorithm 1 gives the complete procedure.

Inference and top-KK selection.

All M=2,398M{=}2{,}398 anchors are evaluated in a single forward pass; candidates are ranked by ‖Δ​x^(k)‖2\|\hat{\Delta x}^{(k)}\|_{2} where Δ​x^(k)≜fθ​(x0(k),s)−x0(k)\hat{\Delta x}^{(k)}\triangleq f_{\theta}(x_{0}^{(k)},s)-x_{0}^{(k)} (lower = higher confidence, anchor close to GT maneuver). The top-KK set is then passed to a downstream cost-based selector decoupled from training, allowing arbitrary cost functions.

4 Experiments

4.1 Setup

Dataset.

Stages 1 and 3 use 120M real-world driving samples covering highway scenarios including intersections, roundabouts, lane changes, and ramp merges; Stage 2 uses a balanced 7.5M subset covering all EF polygon presets. The FPS corpus (100M+ frames, temporally disjoint from training and evaluation) builds 𝒜\mathcal{A}. Evaluation is on ∼\sim2M held-out scenarios with all M=2,398M{=}2{,}398 anchors per scene, yielding an exhaustive trajectory distribution rather than a single-sample estimate.

Metrics.

Trajectory accuracy: for a predicted trajectory a^\hat{a} vs. ground truth a∗a^{*},

ADE=1H​∑t=1H‖a^t−at∗‖2,FDE=‖a^H−aH∗‖2\text{ADE}=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\|\hat{a}_{t}-a^{*}_{t}\|_{2},\qquad\text{FDE}=\|\hat{a}_{H}-a^{*}_{H}\|_{2} (14)

min_ADE/FDE@HH takes the minimum over all 2,398 trajectories (H∈{30,80}H{\in}\{30,80\} = 3 s/8 s) and measures diversity coverage; gt_ADE/FDE uses the nearest-to-GT prediction and measures fidelity—a model can improve min-ADE by spreading trajectories without degrading gt-ADE. Safety: near-range (R<40R{<}40) and far-range (R<80R{<}80) collision rates, where RR is defined in Eq. (10) (fraction of trajectories colliding before step 40 or 80, respectively, aggregated across all MM anchors and all test scenes). Reward: mean RR from Eq. (10) (higher is better).

Baselines.

FM*nn: nn sequential FM inference passes, where each pass feeds the previous output as anchor; FM*2 is the primary non-RL baseline as the second pass consistently reduces min-ADE over FM*1. FMRL*nn: same with RL-fine-tuned FM. EF*1+FM/FMRL*nn: one EF pass prepended. We report original-prior and EF-modified-prior groups separately: cross-group comparisons test whether EF adds corridor controllability while preserving safety, reward, and diversity coverage; within-group comparisons isolate RL or additional FM passes under the same prior type.

Implementation.

All configurations share the same Stage 2 checkpoint. Hyperparameters: N=16N{=}16, λ=0.2\lambda{=}0.2, ϵ0=10−6\epsilon_{0}{=}10^{-6}; top-KK (K=50K{=}50) candidates passed to a downstream cost-based selector. Network architecture details are in Appendix B. At deployment, the full model runs in 2.06 ms/scene on NVIDIA Drive Orin (float16). Training uses PyTorch 2.3, AdamW (lr=10−5=10^{-5}, weight decay 0.010.01, batch 128/GPU); training compute per stage is reported in Appendix E.

4.2 Main Results

FM*2 vs. FMRL*2 (Table 1). FMRL*2 reduces near-range collisions by 89% (27.2%→\to2.9%) and far-range by 87%, while mean reward improves by 32%. GT errors are virtually unchanged (gt_ADE@30: 0.23; gt_ADE@80: 0.42→\to0.40), confirming safety gains preserve imitation quality.

EF-guided configurations. Adding RL (EF*1+FMRL*1) cuts near-range collisions from 13.1% to 4.2% and improves min_ADE@80 (2.35→\to1.25). Compared with FMRL*2, EF*1+FMRL*2 matches performance while adding corridor guidance (near collision: 2.9%→\to1.9%; reward: 78.46→\to78.87; min_ADE@80: 1.34→\to0.95). Higher gt_ADE reflects corridor-oriented anchor relocation, not degradation. In top-5050 (Table 2), FMRL*2 cuts near collision 29×\times (13.7%→\to0.47%).

Qualitative visualization.

Figure 2 shows FM*2 diversity, FMRL*2 safety concentration, EF corridor targeting, and their combination; EF*1+FMRL*2 is in Table 2.

Training dynamics.

All stages converge stably (Figure 3); Stage 3 improves reward while preserving imitation quality (Appendix G.2). The FM loss remains close to the frozen baseline, indicating that reward optimization improves safety without overwriting imitation behavior.

Real-world validation.

DriveAnchor has been tested on public roads (Figure 4), including intersections, pedestrian crossings, and dense merging/lane-change traffic. These tests use the same single-step deployment graph as offline evaluation, including EF rematching before FM decoding.

4.3 Ablation Studies

We ablate the number of ZO gradient neighbors NN in Stage 3 (Appendix F). N=4N{=}4 covers only 4/160=2.5%4/160{=}2.5\% of the 160-D trajectory space and yields near-zero RL progress; N=16N{=}16 performs best and is used throughout. This suggests that modest directional coverage is enough for reward alignment once the anchor vocabulary already provides diverse candidate behaviors.

5 Conclusion and Limitations

DriveAnchor is a composable three-stage framework that grounds behavioral diversity in a structured anchor vocabulary, adds run-time controllability via a geometry-driven Energy Field, and aligns safety through zeroth-order RL—three fully decoupled stages each independently updatable. The FPS-constructed anchor vocabulary converts corpus coverage into deterministic generative diversity; EF injects corridor constraints before generation without differentiable guidance, requiring only EF updates for new presets; deterministic single-step inference reduces reward optimization to a finite-difference direction search in anchor space. The result is an 89% reduction in near-range collision rates and a 32% gain in mean reward, with negligible change in imitation accuracy and 2.06 ms inference on Drive Orin. Post-deployment evaluation on internal production metrics further confirms these gains, with Miles Per Intervention (MPI) and Miles Per Accident (MPA) both consistently improved over the previous production system.

Limitations.

All experiments use an internal dataset without public-benchmark evaluation (nuPlan [4], NAVSIM [11], Waymo [28]). Richer reward signals beyond the current collision-based formulation are an important future direction. The FPS anchor vocabulary’s coverage of rare maneuvers is constrained by the corpus distribution. Finally, the framework omits the explicit flow-time parameter tt, leaving the model’s theoretical convergence properties for future formalization.

Appendix A Discussion: Design Insights and Future Directions

Why rematch after EF relocation.

In the EF+FM pipeline (Eqs. (5)–(6)), EF first displaces the anchor from aanchor∈𝒜a_{\text{anchor}}\in\mathcal{A} to a corridor-guided proposal a~EF=aanchor+Δ​vEF\tilde{a}_{\text{EF}}=a_{\text{anchor}}+\Delta v_{\text{EF}}. Because FM is trained only on the anchor vocabulary and does not cover the whole 160-D trajectory space, the shifted proposal should be rematched to the nearest anchor before decoding. Empirically, feeding the shifted proposal directly into FM degrades kinematic quality, so we project it back to the vocabulary with arematch=arg⁡mina∈𝒜⁡‖a~EF−a‖2a_{\text{rematch}}=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|\tilde{a}_{\text{EF}}-a\|_{2} and then decode with FM. A more principled solution is to align FM training coverage with the anchor partition, motivating the Voronoi-based sampling below.

Toward Voronoi-based anchor sampling.

The rematch step works because FM’s training implicitly partitions trajectory space by proximity to 𝒜\mathcal{A}—an approximation to the natural Voronoi partition of 𝒜\mathcal{A}. The current ϵ\epsilon-ball filter is a hard-radius proxy for this partition: too large a radius causes gradient contamination across maneuver modes; too small leaves the flow field with uncovered regions (Figure 5, left). The principled solution is to compute the exact Voronoi diagram of 𝒜\mathcal{A} and draw each noisy training input from within its Voronoi cell (Figure 5, right). Under this scheme, rematch becomes exact by construction, FM*2 (two sequential passes) directly corresponds to a two-step Euler integration converging toward the exact ODE solution, and kinematic degradation after EF relocation is structurally eliminated.

Temporal guidance for EF.

EF currently enforces only spatial constraints (polygon entry and exit edge). Introducing a time constraint—requiring the trajectory to reach a specified waypoint within T′T^{\prime} seconds—would enable temporal guidance, providing richer behavioral control and finer temporal coordination for timing-critical scenarios such as gap selection during merging. Concretely, a temporal EF could encode a time-budget window alongside the spatial polygon, so that the planner must jointly satisfy both positional and scheduling requirements. This is particularly valuable when spatial feasibility alone is insufficient: for instance, entering a roundabout before an oncoming vehicle closes the gap, or passing an intersection within a traffic-light green phase. The EF specification would then become a compact spatial-temporal contract, and the decoder would need to internalize not only where but also when the trajectory should pass through key regions.

Appendix C GridMap-Accelerated Collision Detection

Naively checking all N+1N{+}1 trajectory–obstacle pairs scales as 𝒪​((N+1)⋅T⋅|𝒪|)\mathcal{O}((N{+}1)\cdot T\cdot|\mathcal{O}|). We use a two-stage strategy: (1) Build: rasterize all obstacles (road boundaries and static objects) into a coarse 2D grid (10​m/cell10\,\text{m/cell}) via Bresenham traversal; each cell stores overlapping obstacle IDs. (2) Query: project each ego OBB onto the grid to retrieve 2–4 candidate obstacles, then apply precise OBB intersection. Per-trajectory early termination skips remaining waypoints once a collision is detected. This substantially reduces per-scene evaluation while preserving exact collision semantics.

Appendix D Stage 2 EF Post-training: Kinematic Constraint Configuration

Two loss forms are used for the six constraints. For upper-bound constraints (speed, acceleration, curvature, lateral acceleration), we use a sigmoid soft penalty that provides a smooth, non-zero gradient just past the boundary (in contrast to a hard hinge):

ℒmax​(x,xmax)=𝔼​[ReLU​(σ​(x−xmax)−0.5)]\mathcal{L}_{\text{max}}(x,\,x_{\max})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\sigma(x-x_{\max})-0.5\right)\right] (15)

where σ\sigma is the sigmoid function. For smoothness constraints (jerk, lateral jerk), we use a ReLU-squared penalty that strongly penalizes large violations:

ℒsmooth(x,xmax)=𝔼[max(0,|x|−xmax)2]\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}(x,\,x_{\max})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max(0,\,|x|-x_{\max})^{2}\right] (16)
